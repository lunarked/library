The setup

Two million people, and only the air routes left open

On 24 June 1948 the Soviet Union closed every road, railway and canal into West Berlin. The city had food for 36 days and coal for 45. Leaving the city was ruled out, and an armed convoy could have started a war. The three air routes into Berlin were still open, so the Allies chose to fly.

On the board, Berlin's airports are drawn as one dock. Each box is a parking spot where a plane is unloaded, like a door at a warehouse. simple model

Lesson 1 · June 194801

Goods have to keep moving

Every plane had to be emptied and sent back fast, because the next one was already on its way. The airports could not work like a warehouse.

Lesson 1 · the problem

A warehouse stores goods before it ships them

In a normal warehouse, goods arrive, go onto a shelf, and wait days or weeks for an order. That takes space, labor and time. Berlin's airports had none of that to spare.

Lesson 1 · the rule
RuleCross-dock

Move goods straight from the incoming vehicle to the outgoing one

A cross-dock is a site where goods go from inbound vehicles straight onto outbound ones, usually within a day, with little or no storage. At Tempelhof, Berliners unloaded each plane onto trucks that drove straight into the city.

Lesson 1 · pros and cons
Cross-dock ruleFlow through, don't store

Good at

  • No storage space or cost
  • Goods move in hours
  • Less handling

Weak at

  • Every arrival must be on time
  • Outbound trucks must be ready
  • One delay backs up the dock
What makes it different: the dock is a place goods pass through, not a place where they wait.
Lesson 2 · July and August 194802

More planes, still not enough

By mid-July the airlift moved about 2,000 tons a day, less than half of what the city needed. More planes arrived, and crews flew whenever they could.

Lesson 2 · the problem

Black Friday: planes stacked up over Berlin

On 13 August 1948 a storm hit Berlin. A plane crashed on landing, many more circled overhead, and loaded planes on the ground could not take off. Adding planes only made the stack in the air bigger.

Lesson 2 · the rule
RuleFind the constraint

Output is set by the dock, not by the fleet

General Tunner, the new head of the airlift, saw that more planes would not help. The limit was how many planes could land, unload and leave each hour.

A cross-dock works the same way. Its output is the number of doors, times the loads each door handles a day, times the size of each load. Extra trucks only wait in the yard. On the board, the spots are the limit.

Lesson 2 · pros and cons
Cross-dock ruleFind the constraint

Good at

  • Shows where to spend
  • Stops buying capacity that waits
  • One number to track

Weak at

  • The limit moves once fixed
  • Needs data on door time
  • Easy to ignore when trucks are cheap
What makes it different: it measures the dock door, the one place every load has to pass.
Lesson 3 · August 194803

Planes arrive in bunches

Some hours brought a crowd of planes and others almost none. In a busy hour, planes waited in the air. In a quiet hour, the unloading crews stood idle.

Lesson 3 · the rule
RuleDock appointments

Give every arrival a fixed time slot

Tunner set one landing every 3 minutes, day and night, in any weather. That is 20 an hour and 480 a day. A pilot who missed his landing flew back with the load instead of circling, so one late plane never held up the rest.

Cross-docks use appointment slots in the same way. Each truck books a time at a door. A truck that misses its slot gets a new one, and is not squeezed in.

Lesson 3 · pros and cons
Cross-dock ruleDock appointments

Good at

  • Steady work for the crews
  • No queue in the yard
  • Easy to plan labor

Weak at

  • A missed slot wastes a trip
  • Carriers have to keep time
  • Less room for rush loads
What makes it different: the dock decides when loads arrive, not the carrier.
Lesson 4 · the air routes04

Traffic in both directions

The slots cleared the sky, but some planes still flew home with their load. Look at the board: planes leaving fly back out the same way the next ones come in.

Lesson 4 · the problem

Incoming and outgoing traffic get in each other's way

When planes going out share the path with planes coming in, every departure can cost an arrival its slot. In a warehouse, the same thing happens when forklifts with incoming and outgoing goods cross the same floor.

Lesson 4 · the rule
RuleOne-way flow

In on two routes, out on the third

There were three air routes into Berlin. Planes flew in on the northern and southern routes and left on the central one, so traffic only moved one way.

Cross-docks are built the same way. Inbound doors sit on one side of a long, narrow building and outbound doors on the other. Bartholdi and Gue found that this I-shape works best for small and mid-size docks, and that very large docks do better as a T or an X.

Lesson 4 · pros and cons
Cross-dock ruleOne-way flow

Good at

  • No crossing paths
  • Fewer delays and accidents
  • Simple rules for drivers

Weak at

  • Longer walks inside the dock
  • Layout is hard to change later
  • Needs space on two sides
What makes it different: it removes the conflict instead of managing it.
Lesson 5 · on the ground05

Each plane sits too long

Crews left the plane to file papers, check the weather and get food. The plane sat at its spot until they came back.

Lesson 5 · the problem

The door is busy, but nothing is moving

A door is only useful while goods are moving through it. Every minute spent waiting for a driver or a form is a minute no load moves. On the board, every spot is full, so the dock cannot take more planes.

Lesson 5 · the rule
RuleCut dwell time

Bring everything to the door

Crews stayed with the plane. An officer brought the return papers and the weather to the door, and a snack truck brought food. Time on the ground in Berlin fell by half, to 30 minutes. Unloading a 10-ton plane fell from 10 minutes to under 6.

With half the time per plane, each spot handles twice the loads. On the board, half the spots are now free for more planes. Modern docks do this by preparing paperwork before the truck arrives and having staff ready at the door.

Lesson 5 · pros and cons
Cross-dock ruleCut dwell time

Good at

  • More output from the same doors
  • Cheap compared with building
  • Frees doors for growth

Weak at

  • Needs staff ready at the door
  • Prep work moves upstream
  • Small delays matter more
What makes it different: it treats the door's time as the scarce resource, not the vehicle's.
Lesson 6 · the planes06

Small loads use up door time

Early on, most planes were small, with 3-ton loads. A small plane takes almost as long to land, park and unload as a large one.

Lesson 6 · the problem

Each slot carried too little

Twenty landings an hour can bring 60 tons or 200 tons. The only difference is the size of each load.

Lesson 6 · the rule
RuleFull, standard loads

Bigger, uniform loads in every slot

Large planes with 10-ton loads replaced the small 3-ton ones. With mostly one type of plane, every crew knew the same routine. By the end of August the airlift delivered more than 4,500 tons a day, enough for the city.

Cross-docks push for full truckloads and standard pallets for the same reason. Each door slot moves more, and every load is handled the same way.

Lesson 6 · pros and cons
Cross-dock ruleFull, standard loads

Good at

  • More tons per slot
  • Fast, routine handling
  • Fewer trips

Weak at

  • Loads wait to fill up
  • Needs standard equipment
  • Less flexible for small orders
What makes it different: it raises the size of each load, not the number of loads.
Lesson 7 · autumn 194807

Winter is coming, and every door is in use

From 20 October the city needed about 5,620 tons a day, mostly coal for the winter. The spots at Tempelhof and Gatow were busy all day.

Lesson 7 · the problem

The doors are already working as fast as they can

Once each door handles loads as fast as it can, with steady slots, short dwell and full loads, the only way to grow is more doors.

Lesson 7 · the rule
RuleAdd doors

Berliners built a third airport in three months

Between August and November 1948, Berliners built a new airport, Tegel. It added a third set of spots, and the planes could come more often.

A growing cross-dock adds doors along its long sides, or opens a second site.

Lesson 7 · pros and cons
Cross-dock ruleAdd doors

Good at

  • More output when doors are full
  • Room for peaks
  • A backup if one site fails

Weak at

  • Costs the most and takes longest
  • Only helps after the other fixes
  • More doors need more staff
What makes it different: it comes last. Building doors costs the most, so first get the most out of the doors you have.
The result · April 194912,941

Easter 1949: the dock at full speed

On 16 April 1949, nearly 1,400 flights brought 12,941 tons in one day, about one landing a minute. After that, the airlift kept up close to 9,000 tons a day.

The end · May 1949

The Soviets reopened the roads

On 12 May 1949 the blockade ended. The airlift kept flying until September to build up stock. In total it carried 2.3 million tons in 277,000 flights, two thirds of it coal. 78 people died in accidents.

Where cross-docks are used today

The same rules run some of the busiest sites in logistics.

Retail

Store deliveries

Suppliers deliver to a cross-dock, and goods are sorted onto trucks for each store the same day. Walmart made this well known in the 1980s.
Freight and parcels

Hubs between routes

Freight and parcel carriers move loads from long-haul trucks to local delivery trucks overnight, with almost no storage.
Factories

Parts to the line

Parts from many suppliers arrive at a cross-dock near the plant, are sorted, and go to the assembly line in the order they are needed.
