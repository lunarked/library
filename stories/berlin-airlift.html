First cross-dock ever: Berlin Airlift ’48

In 1948 the Soviet Union cut every road and railway into West Berlin. The Allies kept the city alive with planes, by running its airports like a cross-dock. This is the question they faced, what they decided, and what happened.

1 · The problem

The Soviets cut every road, railway and canal

On 24 June 1948 the Soviet Union closed all land routes into West Berlin. Two million people had food for 36 days and coal for 45. The city needed about 4,500 tons of supplies a day.

2 · The question the Allies asked

Fight through, leave, or fly?

General Clay wanted an armed convoy on the motorway. Washington and London feared it could start a war. Leaving Berlin was rejected too. The three air routes into the city were still open, so the Allies chose to fly, at first only to buy time.

2 · The question the Allies asked

The first airlift was far too small

The first day brought 80 tons. Small planes carried 3 tons each, crews flew when they could, and planes circled over Berlin waiting to land. By mid-July it moved about 2,000 tons a day, less than half of what the city needed.

So for supply chain, the question is:

How do you move more goods through one dock with no room to store them?

3 · Why it mattered

Berlin's airports worked like a cross-dock

A cross-dock is a site where goods go from incoming vehicles straight onto outgoing ones, with almost no storage. At Tempelhof, planes landed, Berliners unloaded them onto trucks, and the trucks drove into the city. Nothing waited on a shelf.

3 · Why it mattered

The dock, not the number of planes, was the limit

On 13 August 1948 a storm hit Berlin. A plane crashed on landing, many more circled overhead, and loaded planes on the ground could not take off. General Tunner, the new head of the airlift, saw that more planes would not help. The limit was how many could land, unload and leave each hour.

4 · What Tunner decided

Run the airport on a fixed rhythm

One plane landed every 3 minutes, day and night, in any weather. A pilot who missed his landing flew back with the load instead of circling. Planes flew in on two air routes and out on the third, so traffic only moved one way.

4 · What Tunner decided

Keep each plane on the ground as short as possible

Crews stayed with the plane. An officer brought the return papers and the weather to the door, and a snack truck brought food. Large planes with 10-ton loads replaced small 3-ton ones. Time on the ground in Berlin fell by half, to 30 minutes. est. split

5 · What happened

The airlift passed what the city needed

By the end of August it delivered more than 4,500 tons a day. Unloading a 10-ton plane fell from 10 minutes to under 6. Berliners built a new airport, Tegel, in three months. On 16 April 1949 nearly 1,400 flights brought 12,941 tons in one day, and after that the airlift kept up close to 9,000 tons a day.

5 · What happened

The Soviets reopened the roads

On 12 May 1949 the blockade ended. The airlift kept flying until September to build up stock. In total it carried 2.3 million tons, two thirds of it coal. 78 people died in accidents.

The lesson

A dock's output depends on how long each load stays at the door

Fixed arrival slots, standard loads and no waiting at the door let the same space handle far more. Modern cross-docks work the same way: trucks book a door slot, unload and leave.
