Decision story · Airline IT outage, July 2024

How Delta, United and American recovered from the CrowdStrike outage

On 19 July 2024, a faulty software update crashed Windows computers around the world. Delta, United and American all had to stop their flights. Each needed a different number of days to fly normally again.

So for supply chain, the question is:

When your systems go down, how fast can you see where everything is again?

The setup

Three big airlines, one security software

Delta, United and American each fly thousands of flights a day. All three used CrowdStrike's security software on their Windows computers.

On the board, each row is an airline and each column is a day. A bar shows how many flights the airline cancelled that day. The tint shows the share of its schedule: red for 15% or more, amber for some, green for close to normal.

How an airline recovers

Fix the computers, find the crews, rebuild the schedule

Every flight needs a plane and a crew at the same airport, and the crew needs enough legal working hours left. After a big disruption, an airline first restarts its systems. Then it finds out where every plane, pilot and flight attendant is. Then it builds a new schedule that matches them.

Each cancelled flight leaves a plane and a crew in the wrong place. The longer the second step takes, the harder it gets.

Friday 19 JulyFri

The outage

Just after midnight Eastern time, CrowdStrike sends an update to its software on Windows computers. The update has a defect and crashes about 8.5 million of them. CrowdStrike pulls it 78 minutes later, but every crashed computer has to be fixed by hand.

Friday · the problem

All three airlines stop their departures

Early on Friday morning, Delta, United and American all grounded their flights because key systems were down. It was the start of the busiest travel weekend of the summer.

Friday · what American did

Formed one recovery team within an hour

American's chief operating officer said that within an hour of the outage, its operations teams and IT experts were working together to restore systems. Early on Friday it said it was back up and running.

It still cancelled more than 400 flights that day, about 11% of its schedule.

Friday · what United did

Sent technicians to restart its devices

United sent technicians to restart more than 26,000 computers and devices at 365 airports. Staff drove out to sites to keep work going, and some brought their children along.

United cancelled 694 flights, about 22% of its schedule.

Friday · what Delta did

Restarted about 40,000 servers by hand

About 60% of Delta's most important systems, including backups, ran on Windows. Its IT teams had most of them working for the first Atlanta departure at about 7:30 a.m. The app that manages gates took a few more hours.

The crew-tracking tool came back around 11 a.m., but with a backlog of changes. Delta cancelled about 1,200 mainline flights, 32% of its schedule.

Saturday 20 JulySat

Systems are back, but crews are out of place

Most computers work again. But Friday's cancellations left planes and crews in the wrong cities. Now each airline has to find out where everyone is and match them again.

Saturday · American

Close to normal: 44 flights cancelled

American cancelled about 1% of its flights. Its crew-tracking systems kept it aware of where its planes and crews were. Its CEO said keeping track of them was key, so it would not lose the view it needed to recover.

American also had devices to reach staff working out at the airports. Its CEO said its experience with weather disruptions helped too.

American · looking back

What helped

  • Ops and IT in one team within an hour
  • Kept track of planes and crews
  • Devices to reach staff in the field
  • Invested in operations tech after Southwest's 2022 failure

What held it back

  • Ground stop on Friday morning
  • More than 400 flights cancelled on Friday
What made the difference: its systems kept track of where its planes and crews were, so it could rebuild its schedule within a day.
Saturday · United

Fewer cancellations than on Friday

United cancelled about 450 flights, 15% of its schedule, down from 22% on Friday.

Saturday · the problem at Delta

The crew tracker cannot keep up

Every cancelled flight changes where a crew is and how many legal hours it has left. Delta's crew-tracking tool could not process that many changes at once.

Delta cancelled about 1,200 flights again, 36% of its schedule. That was a bigger share than on Friday.

Saturday · what Delta did

Built extra copies of the crew system

To clear the backlog, Delta's IT teams built additional versions of the crew system and used them to work through the changes.

Sunday 21 JulySun

At Delta, one cancellation leads to the next

Crews reached the gate and waited, sometimes for hours, for colleagues who had not arrived. Some ran out of legal working hours. The flight was cancelled, and those crews were now out of place too.

Sunday · what Delta did

Asked crews where they were and offered extra pay

Over the weekend, crew members who logged in were asked to “describe your current location”. Delta offered premium pay to crews who picked up trips, and promised they would get home.

CEO Ed Bastian wrote to customers that a crew-tracking tool was “unable to effectively process the unprecedented number of changes”. Delta cancelled more than 1,000 flights, over 25% of its schedule.

Sunday · United

Down to 262 cancellations

United cancelled 262 flights, about 9% of its schedule. Its CEO said the next day that its systems, tools and schedules had been stable for the last 24 hours.

Sunday · another problem at Delta

Few empty seats to move passengers to

Bastian said Delta's flights were more than 90% booked that weekend. Passengers on a cancelled flight found few open seats on later flights, so some waited days for a new one.

Monday 22 JulyMon

Most airlines are back to normal, Delta is not

Other US airlines cancelled 2% of their flights or fewer. Delta's cancellations were just over half of all airline cancellations in the world that day.

Monday · United

“Our operation is back to normal”

CEO Scott Kirby wrote: “Today, our operation is back to normal and for the last 24 hours our systems, tools and schedules have been stable.” He added that the recovery was “quick given the circumstances but not immediate”. United cancelled 69 flights, about 2%.

United · looking back

What helped

  • Fixed 26,000+ devices on site
  • Teams worked from the same data
  • Mobile devices for staff in the field
  • Recovery process improved before the outage

What held it back

  • Thousands of devices to fix by hand
  • Three days of cancellations
What made the difference: “We all have the same data and we're all looking at the same screen,” said United's IT chief.
Monday · Delta

1,159 cancellations and a queue of 2,000+ items

Delta cancelled 1,159 mainline flights. Its IT chief said the crew-tracking tool had more than 2,000 items waiting to be fixed in its queue. Delta called crew scheduling “deeply complex” and said it needed the most time and manual support to get back in sync.

Tuesday 23 JulyTue

The government opens an investigation

The Department of Transportation opened an investigation into how Delta treated its customers, citing continued widespread disruptions. Passengers had filed more than 5,000 complaints.

Tuesday · Delta

Cancellations fall to about 480

Delta cancelled about 480 mainline flights, 13% of its schedule, and 41% of its flights were delayed. Delta said its “crews are fully staffed and ready”. By its own account it had enough crews, but matching them to the right planes took time.

Tuesday · for customers

Refunds, hotels, meals and miles

Delta offered meal vouchers, hotel rooms and transport where available, plus SkyMiles and travel vouchers as an apology. Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg pressed it to give prompt refunds and free rebooking.

Wednesday 24 JulyWed

Delta is back to normal

Delta cancelled 48 flights, all during the morning start-up, and none later in the day. Phone wait times for reservations fell below two minutes.

Delta · looking back

What helped

  • 40,000 servers restored by hand
  • Extra copies of the crew system
  • Extra pay for crews to pick up trips
  • Hotels, meals and miles for customers

What held it back

  • About 60% of key systems on Windows
  • Crew tracker swamped by changes
  • Flights over 90% booked
What made the difference: for several days Delta could not see where all its crews were, so each cancellation created more work.
The months afterAfter

The cost, and a dispute about the cause

Once flights were back to normal, the airlines added up what the week had cost. Delta and its software suppliers disagreed about why Delta took so much longer.

After · what it cost

About $550 million for Delta, $114 million for United

Delta said the five days cost about $380 million in lost revenue, mostly refunds and compensation in cash and miles, and $170 million in extra costs. About 7,000 of its flights, including its regional partners, and 1.3 million customers were affected.

United later put its own losses at more than $113.6 million, including $20.4 million in compensation for passengers.

After · two explanations

Delta and its suppliers disagree on the cause

Delta said

  • It used CrowdStrike and Microsoft more than other airlines did
  • It has spent billions on IT since 2016
  • “You can't come into a mission-critical 24/7 operation and tell us we have a bug”

CrowdStrike and Microsoft said

  • Delta, unlike its rivals, had not modernized its IT
  • Its crew system was run by IBM and others, not Microsoft
  • Delta turned down their offers of help
After · courts and regulators

A lawsuit, a closed investigation and an insurance claim

In October 2024 Delta sued CrowdStrike for about $550 million. CrowdStrike said its contract caps what it owes at a single-digit million figure. In May 2025 a Georgia judge let Delta's gross negligence claim go ahead.

In June 2026 the Department of Transportation closed its investigation without a fine. It found that refunds had been prompt and told Delta to give customers timely notice of their refund rights. In July 2026 United sued one of its insurers over its own losses.

Why each week looked this way

  • American: its crew-tracking systems kept it aware of where planes and crews were, so it rebuilt its schedule within a day.
  • United: technicians fixed more than 26,000 devices on site, and its teams worked from the same live data. It was back to normal on Monday.
  • Delta: most systems were back on Friday, but its crew tracker fell behind on changes. Crews and planes stayed out of place until Wednesday.

For supply chain

What a supply chain can take from this

Stock, trucks and people go out of place in the same way when the system that tracks them goes down.

Before an outage

Keep a view that does not need the main system

Know where stock, trucks and staff are even when the planning system is down, for example from a daily export or a second tool.
In the first hour

Put operations and IT together

Put operations and IT together, and have everyone work from the same live data, as American and United did.
In the first days

Fix the tracker first

Restore the system that shows where things are before the ones that plan, and add capacity to clear its backlog of changes.
