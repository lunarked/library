Story: Delta vs United, CrowdStrike (2024)
Decision story · IT outage, airline operations

Same outage. United recovered in 3 days. Delta needed 5.

On 19 July 2024 a bad software update crashed Windows computers around the world. Every big US airline was hit. United was back to normal by Monday. Delta kept cancelling for five days, because its crew-scheduling system lost track of its pilots and flight attendants. Here is what happened, why, and when Delta should have stopped patching the schedule and reset it.

Chapter 1 · The race

Friday, 19 July, just after midnight

CrowdStrike, a security company, sent a faulty update to its software on Windows computers. It crashed about 8.5 million of them. CrowdStrike pulled the update 78 minutes later, but every crashed machine had to be fixed by hand. Airports, banks and hospitals went dark. It was the start of the busiest travel weekend of the summer.

Delta and United fly schedules of about the same size. Watch how each one climbs out.

Fri 19 Jul · day 1

Everyone gets hit. Delta cancels about 32% of its flights, United 22%, American 11%. So far, a bad day for all of them.

Sat 20 Jul · day 2

The lines split. United drops to 15%. Delta gets worse: 36%, about 1,200 flights. American is down to 1%.

Sun 21 Jul · day 3

United cancels 262 flights, about 9%. Delta still cancels over 1,000, more than a quarter of its schedule. CEO Ed Bastian apologises: the crew-tracking tool "was unable to effectively process the unprecedented number of changes."

Mon 22 Jul · day 4

United is back: 69 cancellations, 2%. Its CEO writes that "our systems, tools and schedules have been stable" for 24 hours. Delta cancels 1,159.

Tue 23 Jul · day 5

Delta still cancels about 480 flights (13%) and delays 41% more. The US Department of Transportation opens an investigation.

Wed 24 Jul · day 6

Delta is finally back to normal: 48 cancellations, all from the morning start. Final count: about 7,000 Delta flights including its regional partners, against about 1,400 at United.

Chapter 2 · Why Delta stalled

The planes were fine. The crews were lost.

A flight needs a plane, pilots and flight attendants, all in the same city, all with enough legal working hours left. An airline's crew-tracking system holds that puzzle together. When it breaks, the airline can't see who is where.

Normal day

The system knows where every pilot and flight attendant is, how many hours they have left, and what trip they fly next. When a flight is cancelled, it finds a new crew in minutes.

Fri · morning

Delta says about 60% of its key systems run on Windows, and about 40,000 servers had to be reset by hand. The crew tool comes back around 11 a.m., but it is buried under a backlog of changes.

Fri to Sat

Every cancelled flight leaves a crew in the wrong city. Every one of those is a new change for a tool that is already behind. The tool can no longer say where crews are. Crews call in by phone to be reassigned.

Sat to Sun

Here is the trap. A plane waits at the gate for its missing crew. The crew members who are there run out of legal working hours before the rest arrive. The flight is cancelled, and now they are out of position too. It got worse every day. Delta offered crews double pay to pick up trips.

Mon to Tue

Delta said its crews were "fully staffed and ready," and that the crew system was "deeply complex" and needed "the most time and manual support to synchronize." The people were there. The plan didn't know where.

Wed

The tool catches up, crews are back where the schedule expects them, and flights run normally again.

Chapter 3 · Two ways out

Patch the schedule, or start it over

When a schedule breaks, an airline has two choices. It can patch: try to fly each flight, fix crews one by one, and cancel only when it has to. Or it can reset: cancel a big block of flights on purpose, publish a smaller schedule that matches where crews really are, and build back up from there.

✗ Patched for 5 days

Delta

Kept trying to fly as much as it could. Each late cancellation stranded more crews and more customers, and fed the crew tool more changes.
✓ Stable by day 4

United

Fixed more than 26,000 computers by hand at 365 airports and had its crew and schedule tools stable by Sunday night.

The warning was already out there. In December 2022, Southwest's crew system collapsed in a winter storm. It cancelled most of its flights to reset the network. 16,700 flights in total, and a $140 million fine.

So for supply chain, the question is:

When the plan no longer matches reality, when do you stop patching and reset it?

Chapter 4 · You decide

When would you have reset?

You run Delta's operations. A reset means one hard day: about 1,500 flights cancelled on purpose, announced the night before so customers rebook before they reach the airport. Then the schedule rebuilds around where crews really are. Pick your day.

Chapter 5 · The decision

What I would have done

My call

Reset on Saturday. If the crew tool's backlog is still growing 24 hours after the outage, stop patching. Cut the schedule hard for one day and rebuild it from where crews actually are.

The crews were thereDelta had the people. It lost the data about where they were. When the data breaks, a plan built on it stops working, however hard people push.
Every patch makes more workEach late cancellation strands a crew and adds a change to a system that is already behind. Patching fed the problem it was trying to fix.
A late reset is the worst choiceReset early and you trade one hard day for three better ones. Reset late and you get both. Set the trigger before the crisis, not during it.
In supply chain it is the same call: when supplier dates break, do you keep expediting line by line, or freeze and re-plan the build from what you actually have?
The decisions that could have been made
Before

Test the bad day

Stress-test the crew system for a day with thousands of changes. Keep a simple backup for crews to report where they are. Agree in advance what triggers a reset. Southwest 2022 was the warning.
During · 19 to 24 Jul

Call it by Saturday

Watch one number: is the crew backlog shrinking? If not, reset. Protect the big hubs first, pay crews to reposition, and tell customers early so they rebook from home.
After · 2024 on

Fix the weak link

Delta sued CrowdStrike for about $500M; a judge let the main claims go ahead in May 2025. CrowdStrike blamed Delta's old IT. The DOT closed its probe in June 2026 with no fine.

Delta's cost: about $380M in lost revenue (refunds, cash and miles to customers) plus $170M in extra costs (customer expenses, crew), partly offset by $50M of fuel not burned.
