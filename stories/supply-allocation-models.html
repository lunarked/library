The shortage

Four customers ordered 160 chips. There are 100.

The carmaker orders the most and pays the best margin, $50 a unit. The phone maker pays $30 and the distributor $20. The medical device maker is small and pays $40, and its line stops without the part.

Red stripes on the right show each order. Every rule below has to decide how much of each stripe to fill.

The default

First come, first served

With no rule, whoever orders first is filled first. The phone maker and the distributor ordered on Monday. The carmaker and the medical maker ordered on Thursday, when only 20 chips were left. The medical maker gets nothing.

Model family 1 of 7 · Operations research01

Inventory rationing

Hold some stock back for the customers who matter most, before their orders arrive.

Topkis 1968 · Ha 1997 · Clark and Scarf 1960 · Eppen and Schrage 1981
Critical level policy

Below 30 units, only top customers are served

Topkis (1968) and Ha (1997) showed that with several customer classes, a simple threshold often works best. Here the carmaker and the medical maker are top priority. When stock falls to 30 on Monday, the rest of the distributor's order is refused. On Thursday the medical maker is filled in full.

Fair share

The same idea across many warehouses

Clark and Scarf (1960) and Eppen and Schrage (1981) studied how a central warehouse should feed many sites. The common answer in DRP is fair share: send each site enough so all of them run out on the same day.

Family 1 · Pros and cons

Inventory rationing

Good at

  • One simple threshold
  • Protects top customers
  • Easy to run in DRP

Weak at

  • Low-priority buyers turned away
  • Needs a forecast by class
  • Wrong level wastes stock
What makes it different: it looks ahead and saves stock now for important orders that have not arrived yet.
Model family 2 of 7 · Supply chain economics02

Allocation games

Customers react to the rule. Some rules give them a reason to order more than they need.

Cachon and Lariviere 1999 · Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang 1997
Proportional

Everyone gets the same share of its order

100 chips for 160 ordered means each customer gets about 62% of what it asked for. It is easy to explain and looks fair.

The catch

Ordering more always gets you more

Cachon and Lariviere (1999) showed that under a proportional rule, customers gain by inflating orders. If the carmaker orders 96 instead of 60, its share rises from 37 to 49 chips. The others lose, so they inflate too.

Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (1997) called this shortage gaming. It is one of four causes of the bullwhip effect. When supply comes back, the fake orders are cancelled.

Linear

Everyone gives up the same number of chips

The shortage is 60, so each customer gets 15 less than it ordered. Small customers lose the most. The medical maker gets 5 of 20.

Uniform

Equal shares, and spare chips go to the rest

Each customer can get up to an equal share. The medical maker needs only 20, so its spare 5 go to the others, who end up with about 27 each. Ordering more than the equal share gains nothing, so there is little reason to inflate.

Turn-and-earn

Split by each customer's past sales

Carmakers use this with their dealers. Each customer's share follows its share of past sales: 40, 30, 20 and 10%. A bigger order changes nothing. To get more, you have to sell more. Many chip suppliers used this in the 2021 shortage.

Family 2 · Pros and cons

Allocation games

Good at

  • Easy to explain
  • Needs only orders or past sales
  • Uniform and turn-and-earn stop fake orders

Weak at

  • Ignore margin
  • Proportional and linear invite fake orders
  • Past sales lock out new customers
What makes it different: it expects customers to react to the rule, and looks for rules that do not reward fake orders.
Model family 3 of 7 · Airlines03

Revenue management

Keep some supply for higher-margin orders that arrive later.

Littlewood 1972 · Belobaba 1987, 1989 · bid-price controls
Littlewood's rule

Protect chips for the late orders that pay more

Airlines sell cheap seats early and expensive seats late. Littlewood (1972) showed when to stop selling cheap. Keep protecting another unit as long as the chance of selling it at the high price, times the high price, is more than the low price.

Here 70 chips are kept for Thursday's carmaker and medical orders. The phone maker gets the 30 left for Monday, and the distributor gets none. If Thursday had been quiet, some chips would be left unsold. est.

EMSR

Many price classes, each with its own limit

Belobaba (1987) extended the rule to many fare classes, called EMSR. Each class gets a booking limit, and higher classes can always use the space of lower ones.

Bid-price control

Accept an order only if it beats the value of the last unit

Set one number: the bid price, what the last chip is worth to you. Here it is $28. The carmaker, the medical maker and the phone maker are above it and get chips. The distributor at $20 is refused.

Family 3 · Pros and cons

Revenue management

Good at

  • Earns the most per unit
  • Built for uncertain demand
  • Bid price is one number to check

Weak at

  • Turns away loyal low-margin buyers
  • Needs a forecast of late demand
  • Protected chips can go unsold
What makes it different: it decides by the value of each order and the time it arrives.
Model family 4 of 7 · Planning software04

Allocated ATP

Split short supply into quotas by customer group, then promise each order against its group's quota.

Kilger and Meyr · Meyr 2009 · SAP APO and IBP · Kinaxis
Quotas, then promises

Strategic 50, key 35, other 15

Customers are grouped into segments, and each segment gets a quota before orders arrive. The carmaker is strategic and gets 50. The medical maker and the phone maker share the key quota of 35, and the medical maker orders first. The distributor gets the other 15.

The weak spot

Quotas can sit unused while others wait

Quotas are set on a forecast. If the carmaker orders less than planned, its quota sits idle while the phone maker waits. Most systems add rules to borrow from other groups or release unused quota after a set date.

This is the logic behind product allocation in SAP APO and IBP and priority-based allocation in Kinaxis.

Family 4 · Pros and cons

Allocated ATP

Good at

  • Fast, firm promises per order
  • Mixes strategy with daily speed
  • Built into SAP, Kinaxis, Oracle

Weak at

  • Quotas go stale
  • Unused quota strands supply
  • Segments become political
What makes it different: it splits the work in two. Plan the quotas ahead, then promise each order in seconds.
Model family 5 of 7 · Applied math05

Optimization

Write down the goal and the limits, and let a solver find the best split.

LP and MIP · network flow · stochastic programming
Linear programming

The solver fills the best margins first

The goal is the most margin. The limits are 100 chips and no more than each order. The carmaker is filled first, then the medical maker, then the phone maker. The distributor gets nothing.

Add a rule, see its cost

A minimum of 10 for the distributor costs $100

Say the distributor has a contract for at least 10 chips. Add that as a limit. The solver takes 10 from the phone maker, and margin drops from $4,400 to $4,300. The model shows the price of each promise.

Bigger models

Networks, yes/no choices and uncertain demand

Network flow models add plants, warehouses and freight lanes. Mixed-integer programs add whole-number choices, like full trucks. Stochastic programming plans against several demand scenarios at once and may hold a few chips back until demand is clearer.

Family 5 · Pros and cons

Optimization

Good at

  • Best result for the stated goal
  • Handles thousands of parts and sites
  • Shows the cost of each rule

Weak at

  • Only as good as its data
  • Needs experts and software
  • Starves small customers unless told not to
What makes it different: it searches every possible split for the best one, instead of following a fixed rule.
Model family 6 of 7 · Economics and computer science06

Fairness rules

Formal definitions of what a fair split means, so the rule can be explained and defended.

Max-min fairness · proportional fairness (Kelly 1997) · Nash bargaining (Nash 1950)
Max-min fairness

Raise the smallest share as high as it can go

Give the least-served customer as much as possible, then the next, like filling cups with water. The medical maker's cup is full at 20, and the rest settle at about 27. It is the same split as the uniform rule.

Nash bargaining

Start from what each is owed, then share the rest

Nash (1950) defined a fair deal starting from each side's fallback. Here each customer has a contract minimum (the dark marks). They get that first, and the other 60 chips are split equally.

Proportional fairness, from network research (Kelly 1997), depends on how you weigh each customer. Weighed by order size, it gives the proportional split.

Family 6 · Pros and cons

Fairness rules

Good at

  • Clear, defensible definitions
  • Protects small and critical buyers
  • Good for long-term relationships

Weak at

  • Ignores margin
  • You still have to pick a definition
  • Big customers may feel shortchanged
What makes it different: it starts from a definition of fair and lets the math follow from it.
Model family 7 of 7 · Mechanism design07

Auctions and bids

Let price decide who gets supply, instead of a rule set by the seller.

Vickrey 1961 · Myerson 1981
Auction

Fill the highest bids until the chips run out

The carmaker bids $90 a unit, the medical maker $75, the phone maker $55 and the distributor $35. The phone maker gets the last 20 chips, and its bid sets the price at $55 for everyone. The seller keeps the extra that customers were willing to pay.

Mechanism design

Set the rules so telling the truth pays

Vickrey (1961) and Myerson (1981) showed how to design auctions where bidding your true value is your best move. Auctions work best for spot capacity: chip brokers, cloud spot servers, freight spot rates and power markets.

Family 7 · Pros and cons

Auctions and bids

Good at

  • Supply goes to who values it most
  • Price shows how short supply is
  • Hard to game when well designed

Weak at

  • Deep pockets win every time
  • Price spikes hurt trust
  • Rare with long-term customers
What makes it different: the seller sets no priority. Customers show what the part is worth to them through their bids.

How companies combine them

Most companies stack several rules, one for each planning step.

Each week

Set quotas with a model

An LP or MIP splits next week's supply across regions and customer groups.
Each order

Promise against quotas

Allocated ATP checks each order against its group's quota in seconds.
Inside a group

Use a simple rule

Past sales or fair share splits a group's quota, so customers cannot gain by ordering more.
