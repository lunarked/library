The setup

Four customers ordered 160 chips. There are 100.

The carmaker orders the most and pays the best margin, $50 a chip. The phone maker pays $30 and the distributor $20. The medical device maker is small and pays $40, and its line stops without the part.

On the board, each square is 4 chips. Red squares are ordered but not yet filled.

MondayMon

The shortage hits

A supplier problem cuts this week's output to 100 chips. The planner does what most teams do by default and ships orders as they come in.

Monday · the problem

First come, first served stops the medical line

The phone maker and the distributor ordered on Monday morning and are filled in full. The carmaker and the medical maker order on Thursday, when only 20 chips are left. The medical maker gets nothing.

Monday · the fix: inventory rationing

Stop serving low-priority orders below 30 chips

Topkis (1968) and Ha (1997) showed that with several classes of customers, a simple threshold often works best. Serve everyone until stock falls to a set level, then keep the rest for top-priority orders. With a level of 30, the medical maker is filled in full on Thursday.

The same idea runs across warehouses. Clark and Scarf (1960) and Eppen and Schrage (1981) studied how a central warehouse feeds many sites. DRP systems use fair share: each site gets enough so all of them run out on the same day.

Monday · pros and cons

Inventory rationing

Good at

  • One simple threshold
  • Protects top customers
  • Easy to run in DRP

Weak at

  • Low-priority buyers turned away
  • Needs a forecast by class
  • Wrong level wastes stock
What makes it different: it looks ahead and saves stock now for important orders that have not arrived yet.
TuesdayTue

Orders balloon

The phone maker and the distributor complain that the threshold favors others. The planner switches to a split that looks fair to everyone.

Tuesday · the planner tries

Proportional: everyone gets 62% of its order

100 chips for 160 ordered means each customer gets about 62% of what it asked for. It is easy to explain and looks fair.

Tuesday · the problem

The carmaker orders 96 instead of 60

Cachon and Lariviere (1999) showed that under a proportional rule, a customer always gains by ordering more. The carmaker's share rises from 37 to 49 chips. The others lose, so next week they inflate too.

Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (1997) called this shortage gaming. It is one of four causes of the bullwhip effect. When supply comes back, the fake orders are cancelled, and the supplier is left with too much stock.

Tuesday · another try

Linear: everyone gives up 15 chips

The shortage is 60, so each customer gets 15 less than it ordered. Asking for more still pays, and the small medical maker now gets only 5 of 20.

Tuesday · fix one: uniform

Equal shares, capped at each order

Each customer can get up to an equal share. The medical maker needs only 20, so its spare 5 go to the others, who end up with about 27 each. Ordering more than the equal share gains nothing, so the reason to inflate is gone.

Tuesday · fix two: turn-and-earn

Split by past sales, not by orders

Carmakers use this with their dealers. Each customer's share follows its share of past sales: 40, 30, 20 and 10%. A bigger order changes nothing. To get more, a customer has to sell more. Many chip suppliers used this in the 2021 shortage.

Tuesday · pros and cons

Allocation games

Good at

  • Easy to explain
  • Needs only orders or past sales
  • Uniform and turn-and-earn stop fake orders

Weak at

  • Ignore margin
  • Proportional and linear invite fake orders
  • Past sales lock out new customers
What makes it different: it expects customers to react to the rule, and looks for rules that do not reward fake orders.
WednesdayWed

A late order pays more

The carmaker and the medical maker always order late in the week, and they pay the most. If the planner sells everything early, the best orders find an empty shelf. Airlines solved this problem decades ago.

Wednesday · the fix: revenue management

Littlewood's rule: protect chips for the late, high-margin orders

Airlines sell cheap seats early and expensive seats late. Littlewood (1972) showed when to stop selling cheap. Keep protecting another unit as long as the chance of selling it at the high price, times the high price, is more than the low price.

Here 70 chips are kept for Thursday. The phone maker gets the 30 left for early orders, and the distributor gets none. If Thursday had been quiet, some chips would sit unsold. est.

Wednesday · a simpler version

Bid price: accept only orders worth more than $28 a chip

Belobaba (1987) extended Littlewood's rule to many price classes, called EMSR. Bid-price control goes further and uses one number: what the last chip is worth. The carmaker, the medical maker and the phone maker are above $28. The distributor at $20 is refused.

Wednesday · pros and cons

Revenue management

Good at

  • Earns the most per chip
  • Built for uncertain demand
  • Bid price is one number to check

Weak at

  • Turns away loyal low-margin buyers
  • Needs a forecast of late demand
  • Protected chips can go unsold
What makes it different: it decides by the value of each order and the time it arrives.
ThursdayThu

Sales needs promises now

Customers want a firm delivery date the moment they order. The planner cannot hold a meeting for every order, so the rules have to live in the planning system.

Thursday · the fix: allocated ATP

Quotas by customer group, set before orders arrive

Kilger and Meyr describe allocated ATP. Customers are grouped into segments, and each segment gets a quota of the short supply: strategic 50, key 35, other 15. Each order is promised in seconds against its group's quota. The medical maker orders first in the key group and takes 20, so the phone maker gets 15.

This is the logic behind product allocation in SAP APO and IBP and priority-based allocation in Kinaxis.

Thursday · the catch

Quotas can sit unused while others wait

Quotas are set on a forecast. If the carmaker orders less than planned, its quota sits idle while the phone maker waits. Most systems add rules to borrow from other groups or release unused quota after a set date. Meyr (2009) found that segmenting this way can still earn more than first come, first served.

Thursday · pros and cons

Allocated ATP

Good at

  • Fast, firm promises per order
  • Mixes strategy with daily speed
  • Built into SAP, Kinaxis, Oracle

Weak at

  • Quotas go stale
  • Unused quota strands supply
  • Segments become political
What makes it different: it splits the work in two. Plan the quotas ahead, then promise each order in seconds.
FridayFri

Finance wants the most margin

The CFO asks for the split that earns the most. That is a job for a solver.

Friday · the fix: optimization

A linear program fills the best margins first

The goal is the most margin. The limits are 100 chips and no more than each order. The solver fills the carmaker, then the medical maker, then the phone maker. Margin reaches the maximum of $4,400, and the distributor gets nothing.

Friday · add a rule, see its cost

A minimum of 10 for the distributor costs $100

The distributor has a contract for at least 10 chips. Add that as a limit. The solver takes 10 from the phone maker, and margin drops to $4,300. The model shows the price of each promise.

Bigger models add plants, warehouses and freight lanes (network flow), yes/no choices like full trucks (MIP), and several demand scenarios at once (stochastic programming).

Friday · pros and cons

Optimization

Good at

  • Best result for the stated goal
  • Handles thousands of parts and sites
  • Shows the cost of each rule

Weak at

  • Only as good as its data
  • Needs experts and software
  • Starves small customers unless told not to
What makes it different: it searches every possible split for the best one, instead of following a fixed rule.
SaturdaySat

"This is unfair"

The distributor's CEO calls. They have bought from this supplier for 20 years and now get a third of their order. The planner needs a split that can be defended in plain words.

Saturday · fix one: max-min fairness

Raise the smallest share as high as it can go

Give the least-served customer as much as possible, then the next, like filling cups with water. The medical maker's cup is full at 20, and the rest settle at about 27. It is the same split as Tuesday's uniform rule.

Saturday · fix two: Nash bargaining

Start from what each is owed, then share the rest

Nash (1950) defined a fair deal starting from each side's fallback. Here each customer first gets its contract minimum: 20, 10, 5 and 5. The other 60 chips are split equally.

Proportional fairness, from network research (Kelly 1997), weighs each customer. Weighed by order size, it gives Tuesday's proportional split.

Saturday · pros and cons

Fairness rules

Good at

  • Clear, defensible definitions
  • Protects small and critical buyers
  • Good for long-term relationships

Weak at

  • Ignores margin
  • You still have to pick a definition
  • Big customers may feel shortchanged
What makes it different: it starts from a definition of fair and lets the math follow from it.
SundaySun

A broker calls

A broker offers $90 a chip for anything the planner can spare. It raises a question the rules so far have avoided: why not let price decide?

Sunday · the option: an auction

Fill the highest bids until the chips run out

The carmaker bids $90, the medical maker $75, the phone maker $55 and the distributor $35. The phone maker gets the last 20 chips, and its bid sets the price at $55 for everyone. Every buyer pays $55 a chip, more than it pays under any fixed rule.

Vickrey (1961) and Myerson (1981) showed how to design auctions where bidding your true value is your best move.

Sunday · pros and cons

Auctions and bids

Good at

  • Supply goes to who values it most
  • Price shows how short supply is
  • Hard to game when well designed

Weak at

  • Deep pockets win every time
  • Price spikes hurt trust
  • Rare with long-term customers
What makes it different: the seller sets no priority. Customers show what the part is worth through their bids. It fits spot capacity better than long-term customers.

How companies combine them

Most companies stack several rules, one for each planning step.

Each week

Set quotas with a model

An LP or MIP splits next week's supply across regions and customer groups.
Each order

Promise against quotas

Allocated ATP checks each order against its group's quota in seconds.
Inside a group

Use a simple rule

Past sales or fair share splits a group's quota, so customers cannot gain by ordering more.
