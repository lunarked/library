Story: Supply allocation models
Explainer · Allocating scarce supply

Who gets the parts when there are not enough?

When supply runs short, someone has to decide who gets what. Researchers in several fields have studied this problem. This story walks through each family of models with one example and shows what each one is good and bad at.

The problem

There are 100 chips this week and orders for 160

A chip maker has 100 units to ship. Its customers ordered 160. It cannot make more this week, so someone has to decide who gets the 100.

The customers

Four customers, each different

The carmaker orders the most and pays the best margin. The phone maker and the distributor earn less per unit. The medical device maker is small, but its line stops without the part. We use these same four in every chapter.

The theory

Seven families of models give different answers

The ideas come from operations research, economics, airlines, planning software, math and fairness theory. Each chapter covers one family: how it works, a picture of the split, and what it is good and bad at.

So for supply chain, the question is:

When there is not enough for everyone, who should get it?

01
Operations research

Inventory rationing

Hold some stock back for the customers who matter most, before their orders arrive.

Topkis 1968 · Ha 1997 · Clark and Scarf 1960 · Eppen and Schrage 1981
Critical level policy

Below a set level, only top customers get stock

Topkis (1968) and Ha (1997) showed that with several customer classes, a simple threshold often works best. You serve everyone until stock falls to a set level. Below it, only high-priority orders are filled.

Here the carmaker and the medical maker are top priority. On Wednesday stock reaches 30, so the next distributor order is refused.

Setting the level

The level balances two costs

Set it too low and a top customer runs short. Set it too high and you turn away good sales and keep stock nobody uses. The right level depends on how much more a top order is worth and how much top demand you expect before the next delivery.

Multi-echelon allocation

A central warehouse splits stock across sites

Clark and Scarf (1960) and Eppen and Schrage (1981) studied how a central warehouse should feed many sites. Here the warehouse has 90 units and three sites with very different stock.

Fair share

Every site ends up with the same days of stock

Give each site enough so that all of them run out on the same day. Site 3 gets the most because it had nothing. This is the fair share logic in DRP systems.

Where you see it

Spare parts, service contracts and store replenishment

Airlines keep spare parts for planes that cannot fly, and refuse routine orders when stock is low. Retail DRP uses fair share when a warehouse cannot fill every store.

02
Supply chain economics

Allocation games

Customers react to the rule. Some rules give them a reason to order more than they need.

Cachon and Lariviere 1999 · Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang 1997
Proportional

Everyone gets the same share of what they ordered

100 units for 160 ordered means each customer gets about 62% of its order. It is easy to explain and looks fair. Red stripes show the part of each order that is not filled.

The catch

Ordering more always gets you more

Cachon and Lariviere (1999) showed that under a proportional rule, customers gain by inflating orders. If the carmaker orders 96 instead of 60, its share rises from 37 to 49 units. The others lose, so they inflate too.

Shortage gaming

Fake orders make the shortage look bigger than it is

Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (1997) called this shortage gaming. It is one of four causes of the bullwhip effect. Orders jump far above real need. When supply comes back, customers cancel, and the supplier is left with too much stock and too much capacity.

Linear

Everyone gives up the same number of units

The shortage is 60 units, so each of the four gets 15 less than it ordered. Small customers lose the most. The medical maker gets only 5 of 20.

Uniform

Split equally, and pass on what small customers do not need

Each customer can get up to an equal share. The medical maker needs only 20, so its spare 5 go to the others, who end up with about 27 each. Ordering more than the equal share gains nothing, so there is little reason to inflate.

Turn-and-earn

Split by each customer's past sales

Carmakers use this with their dealers. A dealer's share of new cars follows its share of past sales. A bigger order changes nothing. To get more, a dealer has to sell more.

Where you see it

Car dealers, chip shortages and new consoles

In the 2021 chip shortage, many suppliers put customers "on allocation" and capped orders at a share of past purchases. That is turn-and-earn used to stop double ordering.

03
Revenue management, from airlines

Revenue management

Keep some supply for higher-margin orders that arrive later.

Littlewood 1972 · Belobaba 1987, 1989 · bid-price controls
Littlewood's rule

Protect units for late orders that pay more

Airlines sell cheap seats early and expensive seats late. Littlewood (1972) showed when to stop selling cheap. Keep protecting another unit as long as the chance of selling it at the high price, times the high price, is more than the low price.

In our example

Protect 30 units for the carmaker

The distributor orders early at $20 a unit. The carmaker orders late at $50. $20 is 40% of $50, so you keep protecting units while the chance the carmaker needs them is above 40%. Here that is about 30 units. Some may go unsold. est.

EMSR

Many price classes, each with its own limit

Belobaba (1987) extended the rule to many fare classes, called EMSR. Each class gets a booking limit. Higher classes can always use the space of lower ones, so the limits are nested.

Bid-price control

Accept an order only if it beats the value of the last unit

Instead of limits per class, set one number: the bid price. It is what the last unit is worth to you, given what could still come. Here it is $28. The carmaker, the medical maker and the phone maker are accepted. The distributor at $20 is refused.

Where you see it

Airlines, hotels and factory capacity

Airlines and hotels run this every second. In manufacturing, it shows up as order acceptance: a plant takes a rush order only if its margin beats the value of the capacity it uses.

04
Planning software (APS)

Allocated ATP

Split short supply into quotas by customer group, then promise each order against its group's quota.

Kilger and Meyr · Meyr 2009 · SAP APO and IBP · Kinaxis
Segment and pre-allocate

Give each customer group a quota

Kilger and Meyr describe allocated ATP (aATP). First you group customers into segments. Then you give each segment a quota of the short supply before orders come in. Here: strategic 50, key 35 and other 15.

Promise against the quota

Each order uses up its group's quota

The carmaker gets 50 of 60. In the key group, the medical maker orders first and takes 20, so the phone maker gets the last 15. The distributor gets 15 of 30.

The weak spot

Quotas can sit unused while others wait

Quotas are set on a forecast. If the carmaker orders less than planned, its quota sits idle. Most systems add rules: let an order borrow from another group, or release unused quota after a set date.

Where you see it

Product allocation in SAP, priority allocation in Kinaxis

This is the logic behind product allocation in SAP APO and IBP and priority-based allocation in Kinaxis. Meyr (2009) found that segmenting customers this way can earn more than first come, first served.

05
Mathematical optimization

Optimization models

Write down the goal and the limits, and let a solver find the best split.

Linear and mixed-integer programs · network flow · stochastic programming
Linear programming

The solver fills the best margins first

An LP model picks the split that earns the most, within the limits you set. Here the limits are 100 units in total and no more than each order. The carmaker is filled first, then the medical maker, then the phone maker. The distributor gets nothing.

Add a rule, see its cost

A minimum for the distributor costs $100

Say the distributor has a contract for at least 10 units. Add that as a limit. The solver takes 10 from the phone maker, and margin drops from $4,400 to $4,300. The model shows the price of every promise you make.

Networks and MIP

Real cases have many plants, lanes and yes/no choices

Network flow models send supply from plants through warehouses to customers at the lowest cost or highest margin. Mixed-integer programs (MIP) add whole-number choices, such as full truckloads or which line to run.

Stochastic programming

Plan for several futures at once

The LP assumes orders are known. Stochastic programming plans against several demand scenarios and their odds. It may hold a few units back, or send them where they are most likely to be needed.

Where you see it

S&OP tools and supply planning engines

Planning tools use LP and MIP to set weekly allocations across plants and regions. The quotas in chapter 4 are often the output of a model like this.

06
Economics and computer science

Fairness rules

Formal definitions of what a fair split means, so the rule can be explained and defended.

Max-min fairness · proportional fairness (Kelly 1997) · Nash bargaining (Nash 1950)
Max-min fairness

Raise the smallest share as high as it can go

Give the least-served customer as much as possible, then the next, and so on. It works like filling cups with water. The medical maker's cup is full at 20, and the rest settle at about 27. This is the same split as the uniform rule in chapter 2.

Nash bargaining

Start from what each is owed, then share the rest

Nash (1950) defined a fair deal starting from each side's fallback. Here each customer has a contract minimum. They get that first, and the remaining 60 units are split equally, up to each order.

Proportional fairness

Each definition gives a different split

Proportional fairness comes from network research (Kelly 1997). It depends on how you weigh each customer. Weighed by order size, it gives the proportional split from chapter 2. There is no single fair answer, so the choice of definition is a business decision.

Where you see it

Internet traffic, public supplies and shared capacity

Networks share bandwidth with max-min and proportional fairness. Governments used similar rules to split vaccines and medical supplies across regions in 2020 and 2021.

07
Mechanism design

Auctions and bids

Let price or bids decide who gets supply, instead of a rule set by the seller.

Vickrey 1961 · Myerson 1981
Auction

Fill the highest bids until supply runs out

Each customer bids what a unit is worth to it. Sort the bids from high to low and fill them until the 100 units are gone. Here the carmaker bids $90, the medical maker $75, the phone maker $55 and the distributor $35.

The result

The same split as the margin model, at a higher price

The phone maker gets 20 of its 50, and its bid sets the price at $55. The distributor gets nothing. The seller earns the extra that customers were willing to pay.

Mechanism design

Design the rules so telling the truth pays

Vickrey (1961) and Myerson (1981) showed how to set auction rules so that bidding your true value is your best move. In a second-price auction you pay the next highest bid, so there is no reason to bid low.

When to use it

Best for spot capacity on top of contracts

Most suppliers will not auction parts to their main customers, because the price spike damages the relationship. Auctions work best for spare or spot capacity on top of contracts.

How companies combine them

Most companies do not pick one rule. They stack them, one for each planning step.

Each week

Set quotas with a model

An LP or MIP splits next week's supply across regions and customer groups.
Each order

Promise against quotas

Allocated ATP checks each order against its group's quota in seconds.
Inside a group

Use a simple rule

Past sales or fair share splits a group's quota, so customers cannot gain by ordering more.
